### Standard Deduction (2025)

- **Single:** $15,000
- **Married Filing Jointly:** $30,000
- **Head of Household:** $22,500
- **Additional (Age 65+ or Blind):** $1,550 single, $1,250 MFJ (per person)

### Understanding Marginal vs Effective Tax Rate

**Marginal Rate:** Tax rate on next dollar earned

**Effective Rate:** Total tax ÷ total income

**Example:**
```
Married Filing Jointly
Gross Income: $150,000
Standard Deduction: -$30,000
Taxable Income: $120,000

Tax Calculation:
10% on first $23,850 = $2,385
12% on next $73,100 = $8,772
22% on remaining $23,050 = $5,071
Total Tax: $16,228

Marginal Rate: 22% (bracket of last dollar)
Effective Rate: $16,228 ÷ $150,000 = 10.8%
```

### Bracket Management Strategies

**Stay in 12% Bracket:**
- MFJ can have up to ~$127,000 gross income and stay in 12%
- Ideal for Roth conversions (low tax cost)
- Maximize pre-tax 401k to reduce taxable income to top of 12%

**Avoid 32% Bracket:**
- Large jump from 24% to 32%
- Defer income if close to threshold
- Consider timing of capital gains, bonuses, Roth conversions

**Example - Bracket Management:**
```
Current Income: $200,000 (MFJ)
Standard Deduction: $30,000
Taxable Income: $170,000 (in 22% bracket)

Option 1: Maximize 401(k)
- Contribute $23,500 to 401(k)
- New Taxable Income: $146,500 (still 22%)
- Tax Savings: $23,500 × 22% = $5,170

Option 2: Max 401(k) + HSA + Small Roth Conversion
- 401(k): $23,500
- HSA: $8,550 (family)
- Taxable Income: $138,000
- Room left in 22% bracket: $68,700
- Roth Conversion: $60,000 (stay in 22%)
- Pay 22% tax on conversion now, avoid 24%+ later
```

## Tax-Deferred vs Tax-Free vs Taxable Accounts

### Account Type Comparison

| Feature | Tax-Deferred (Traditional IRA/401k) | Tax-Free (Roth IRA/401k) | Taxable (Brokerage) |
|---------|-----------------------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Contribution | Pre-tax (deductible) | After-tax | After-tax |
| Growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-free | Taxed annually |
| Withdrawal | Fully taxable | Tax-free (qualified) | Cap gains rates |
| RMDs | Yes, starting age 73-75 | No (Roth IRA only) | No |
| Early Withdrawal Penalty | Yes, 10% before 59½ | Yes on earnings | No |
| Contribution Limits | Yes ($23,500/yr 401k, $7,000 IRA) | Yes (same limits) | No |
| Income Limits | No (but deductibility phases out) | Yes | No |

### Traditional vs Roth Decision Framework

**Choose Traditional (Pre-Tax) If:**
- Currently in high tax bracket (24%+)
- Expect lower bracket in retirement
- Need tax deduction now
- Close to retirement (short time horizon)
- Maxing contributions (pre-tax allows larger contribution)

**Choose Roth (After-Tax) If:**
- Currently in low tax bracket (12% or less)
- Expect higher bracket in retirement
- Young with long time horizon (decades of tax-free growth)
- Want tax diversification
- Want to avoid RMDs
- Estate planning (tax-free inheritance)

**Example Decision:**

**Client A - Age 30, Income $70,000 (MFJ with spouse):**
- In 12% tax bracket
- 35 years to retirement
- **Recommendation: Roth 401(k) and Roth IRA**
- Rationale: Low bracket now, decades of tax-free growth, likely higher bracket later

**Client B - Age 55, Income $400,000 (MFJ):**
- In 35% tax bracket
- 10 years to retirement
- Expect $150,000 income in retirement (24% bracket)
- **Recommendation: Traditional 401(k)**
- Rationale: Save 35% now, pay 24% later (11% arbitrage)

**Client C - Age 45, Income $180,000 (MFJ):**
- In 22% tax bracket
- Mixed situation
- **Recommendation: Split between Traditional and Roth**
- Rationale: Tax diversification, flexibility in retirement

### Tax Diversification Strategy

**Goal:** Have money in all three account types for withdrawal flexibility

**Ideal Retirement Tax Diversification:**
- **Tax-Deferred (Traditional):** 50-60% of retirement savings
  - Use to fill lower brackets in retirement
- **Tax-Free (Roth):** 20-30% of retirement savings
  - Use when income would push into higher bracket
- **Taxable:** 10-20% of retirement savings
  - Use for bridge years, qualified dividends, capital gains at 0%

**Benefits:**
- Flexibility to manage taxable income in retirement
- Ability to "fill brackets" efficiently
- Options for large one-time expenses
- Estate planning flexibility

**Example Retirement Withdrawal with Tax Diversification:**
```
Retirement Income Need: $120,000/year
Social Security: $40,000 (85% taxable = $34,000)

Withdrawal Strategy (MFJ):
- Fill 12% bracket with Traditional IRA: $60,000
  - Taxable income: $94,000 (close to top of 12%)
- Additional from Roth IRA: $20,000 (tax-free, doesn't add to taxable income)
- Total Income: $120,000
- Total Taxable Income: $94,000
- Effective Tax Rate: ~9%

vs No Tax Diversification (all Traditional):
- Traditional IRA withdrawal: $80,000
- Taxable income: $114,000 (into 22% bracket)
- Effective Tax Rate: ~12%
- Higher Medicare IRMAA, more SS taxed
```

## Retirement Account Tax Strategies

### Roth Conversion Timing

**Best Years for Roth Conversions:**

**1. Early Retirement (before SS and RMDs)**
- Ages 60-72: Gap between retirement and RMDs
- Lower income, lower bracket
- Convert Traditional to Roth at low rates

**2. Low-Income Year**
- Job loss or sabbatical
- Business downturn
- One spouse retires before the other

**3. Market Decline**
- Convert more shares for same tax cost
- Recovery happens in Roth (tax-free)

**4. Before Age 63**
- Avoid Medicare IRMAA
- IRMAA based on income 2 years prior
- Keep income low in years that will affect Medicare premiums

**Roth Conversion Analysis:**

```
Age 62, Retired, Before Social Security
Current Bracket: 12%
Expected Bracket at RMD Age (73): 24%

Analysis:
- Convert $100,000 now in 12% bracket
- Tax cost: $12,000
- Alternative: Wait and withdraw at 24% bracket
- Tax cost: $24,000
- **Savings: $12,000** (plus tax-free growth in Roth)

Additionally:
- Reduces future RMDs (less taxable income at 73+)
- Reduces Social Security taxation
- Avoids Medicare IRMAA
- Tax-free inheritance for heirs
```

### Backdoor Roth IRA

**For High Earners Above Roth IRA Income Limits:**

**Process:**
1. Contribute to non-deductible Traditional IRA ($7,000, or $8,000 age 50+)
2. Immediately convert to Roth IRA
3. Pay tax on any earnings since contribution (minimal if immediate)
4. Now have Roth IRA contribution despite income limits

**Pro-Rata Rule Complication:**
- If have existing pre-tax IRA balances, pro-rata rule applies
- Conversion is proportionally taxable based on ratio of after-tax to total IRA balance

**Example Without Pro-Rata Issue:**
```
No existing Traditional IRA balance
Contribute $7,000 non-deductible to Traditional IRA
Immediately convert to Roth
Tax: $0 (all after-tax basis)
Result: $7,000 in Roth IRA ✅
```

**Example With Pro-Rata Issue:**
```
Existing Traditional IRA: $93,000 (all pre-tax)
Contribute $7,000 non-deductible
Total IRA: $100,000 (7% after-tax, 93% pre-tax)

Convert $7,000 to Roth:
- 7% is tax-free (after-tax basis): $490
- 93% is taxable: $6,510
- Taxed at ordinary rates

Result: $7,000 in Roth, but $6,510 taxable ❌ (not ideal)
```

**Solutions for Pro-Rata Problem:**
- Rollover existing Traditional IRA to employer 401(k) (if plan accepts)
- Convert entire IRA balance to Roth (pay tax)
- Keep traditional IRA separate, skip backdoor Roth

### Mega Backdoor Roth

**For High Earners with Supportive 401(k) Plans:**

**Requirements:**
- Employer 401(k) allows after-tax contributions (beyond $23,500 limit)
- Plan allows in-service distributions or in-plan Roth conversions

**Process:**
1. Max regular 401(k): $23,500
2. Receive employer match: (varies, assume $7,500)
3. Contribute after-tax to 401(k): Up to $39,000 (to reach $70,000 total limit)
4. Immediately convert after-tax to Roth 401(k) or rollover to Roth IRA
5. Earnings on after-tax are taxable upon conversion (minimal if immediate)

**Example:**
```
2025 Contribution Limits:
- Employee elective deferral: $23,500
- Employer match: $7,500
- Total so far: $31,000
- IRS limit: $70,000
- Room for after-tax: $39,000

Mega Backdoor Roth:
- Contribute $39,000 after-tax
- Immediately convert to Roth
- Result: $39,000 additional Roth contribution (beyond $7,000 IRA limit)
- Total Roth for year: $46,000 (Mega Backdoor + Backdoor Roth IRA)
```

**Benefit:** Allows very high retirement savings in tax-advantaged accounts

### Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

**RMD Ages (SECURE Act 2.0):**
- Born 1951-1959: RMD starts age 73
- Born 1960+: RMD starts age 75

**RMD Calculation:**
```
RMD = IRA Balance (Dec 31 prior year) ÷ Life Expectancy Factor

Example:
Age 73, IRA Balance $1,000,000
Life Expectancy Factor: 26.5 (from IRS Uniform Lifetime Table)
RMD: $1,000,000 ÷ 26.5 = $37,736
```

**Strategies to Reduce RMDs:**

**1. Roth Conversions Before RMD Age**
- Reduces Traditional IRA balance
- Lower future RMDs
- No RMDs on Roth IRA during lifetime

**2. Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs)**
- Age 70½+: Up to $105,000/year (2024, indexed)
- Direct transfer from IRA to charity
- Counts toward RMD
- Not included in taxable income (better than itemized deduction)

**3. Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract (QLAC)**
- Purchase annuity inside IRA
- Up to $200,000 or 25% of IRA (whichever less)
- Defers RMDs on that portion until age 85
- Reduces age 73-84 RMDs

**QCD Example:**
```
Age 75, RMD $50,000
Charitably inclined, normally donate $20,000/year

Option 1: Traditional approach
- Take $50,000 RMD (taxable)
- Donate $20,000 cash (itemized deduction if exceeds standard deduction)
- AGI: $50,000 (affects Medicare IRMAA, SS taxation)
- Taxable income: $30,000 (if can itemize)

Option 2: QCD
- QCD $20,000 directly to charity
- Take remaining $30,000 RMD
- AGI: $30,000 (lower AGI = better)
- Taxable income: $30,000
- Donation completed without needing to itemize

QCD is superior: Lower AGI, works with standard deduction, reduces Medicare IRMAA
```

### Inherited IRA Rules (SECURE Act 2.0)

**Death Before 2020 (Old Rules):**
- Beneficiaries could "stretch" distributions over their lifetime
- Minimal annual RMDs based on beneficiary's life expectancy

**Death After 2019 (New Rules):**
- Most beneficiaries must distribute entire IRA within 10 years
- No annual RMDs, but must empty account by end of year 10

**Exceptions (Can Still Stretch):**
- Surviving spouse
- Minor children (until age of majority, then 10-year rule)
- Disabled or chronically ill individuals
- Beneficiaries within 10 years of age of decedent

**Tax Planning for Inherited IRAs:**

**For Beneficiaries (Non-Spouse):**
- Plan distributions over 10 years to minimize tax impact
- Avoid taking entire balance in one year (huge tax hit)
- Consider spreading over lower-income years
- If in high bracket now, delay distributions until retirement

**For Original Account Owner:**
- Roth conversions reduce tax burden on heirs
- Inherited Roth IRA still has 10-year rule but all distributions tax-free
- Consider whether to leave Traditional (heirs pay tax) or Roth (you pay tax)

**Example Inherited IRA Strategy:**
```
Inherit $500,000 Traditional IRA
Beneficiary age 45, income $150,000/year (24% bracket)

Bad Strategy: Take $500,000 in year 1
- Income: $150,000 + $500,000 = $650,000
- Federal tax: ~$185,000 (massive tax hit into 37% bracket)

Good Strategy: Spread over 10 years
- Years 1-9: Take $0 (let it grow)
- Year 10: Take $500,000+growth
- Still large tax, but better than year 1

Best Strategy: Plan around income
- Years with lower income (e.g., sabbatical, early retirement): Take larger distributions
- Years with higher income: Take minimal or nothing
- Year 10: Take remainder
- Optimize to stay in lower brackets
```

## Capital Gains and Tax-Loss Harvesting

### Capital Gains Tax Rates (2025)

**Long-Term Capital Gains (held >1 year):**

| Taxable Income (Single) | Taxable Income (MFJ) | LTCG Rate |
|------------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| $0 - $48,350 | $0 - $96,700 | 0% |
| $48,351 - $533,400 | $96,701 - $600,050 | 15% |
| $533,401+ | $600,051+ | 20% |

**Plus Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** 3.8% on investment income if MAGI >$200k single, $250k MFJ

**Short-Term Capital Gains (held ≤1 year):**
- Taxed as ordinary income at marginal rates (10% - 37%)

### 0% Capital Gains Strategy

**Opportunity:** Realize capital gains at 0% tax

**Who Qualifies:**
- Retirees with low income (under ~$97k MFJ)
- Early retirees before Social Security
- Anyone with temporary low-income year

**Strategy:**
1. Identify appreciated positions in taxable account
2. Sell to realize long-term capital gains
3. Immediately repurchase (no wash sale rule for gains)
4. Step up cost basis
5. Reset holding period

**Example:**
```
Retired couple, age 62
Income: $60,000 (Traditional IRA withdrawal)
Standard Deduction: $30,000
Taxable Income: $30,000

Available room in 0% LTCG bracket:
- Top of 0% bracket: $96,700
- Current taxable income: $30,000
- Available room: $66,700

Strategy:
- Sell stock with $66,000 long-term capital gain
- Pay 0% federal tax on gain
- Immediately repurchase stock
- New cost basis: Old basis + $66,000
- Future sale: Only gain above new basis is taxable

Benefit: Realized $66,000 gain tax-free, stepped up basis
```

**Annual Opportunity:**
- Repeat each year in retirement
- Gradually step up basis on all appreciated positions
- Reduce future capital gains tax

### Tax-Loss Harvesting

**Concept:** Sell investments at a loss to offset capital gains and up to $3,000 of ordinary income

**Benefits:**
- Offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar
- Offset up to $3,000 ordinary income annually
- Excess losses carry forward indefinitely
- Maintain market exposure by purchasing similar (not identical) security

**Wash Sale Rule:**
- Cannot buy "substantially identical" security 30 days before or after sale
- 61-day window (30 days before, day of sale, 30 days after)
- Violating wash sale rule disallows loss

**Tax-Loss Harvesting Process:**

1. **Identify Losses:**
   - Review taxable account for positions with unrealized losses
   - Focus on long-term positions (held >1 year)

2. **Sell at Loss:**
   - Execute sale to realize loss

3. **Replace Position:**
   - Wait 31 days (avoid wash sale), OR
   - Buy similar but not identical security immediately

4. **Use Loss:**
   - Offset capital gains first
   - Offset up to $3,000 ordinary income
   - Carry forward excess losses

**Example:**
```
Realized Capital Gains (from rebalancing): $20,000
Ordinary Income: $150,000

Identify Loss Position:
- Vanguard Total Stock (VTI): Purchased at $100,000, now worth $85,000
- Unrealized loss: $15,000

Tax-Loss Harvest:
- Sell VTI: Realize $15,000 loss
- Immediately buy Schwab Total Stock (SCHB): Similar but not identical, maintain market exposure
- After 31 days, can switch back to VTI if desired

Tax Benefit:
- Capital gains: $20,000
- Capital losses: -$15,000
- Net capital gain: $5,000
- Taxed at 15% LTCG rate: $750 tax
- Without harvesting: $20,000 × 15% = $3,000 tax
- **Tax Savings: $2,250**

Plus: Reset cost basis on SCHB to $85,000 (current value), may create future harvesting opportunity
```

**Replacement Strategies to Avoid Wash Sale:**

| Original Fund | Replacement Fund |
|--------------|------------------|
| VTI (Total Stock Market) | SCHB or ITOT |
| VOO (S&P 500) | IVV or SPLG |
| VEA (Developed Intl) | IEFA or SCHF |
| VWO (Emerging Markets) | IEMG or SCHE |
| BND (Total Bond) | AGG or SCHZ |

**Sector Rotation Alternative:**
- Sell Technology ETF, buy S&P 500 (different enough, no wash sale)
- Sell Small Cap Value, buy Small Cap Growth
- Reduces tracking error risk

### Qualified Dividends and Tax Efficiency

**Qualified Dividends:**
- Taxed at long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, 20%)
- Must meet holding period (>60 days during 121-day period around ex-dividend date)
- Must be from U.S. corporation or qualified foreign corporation

**Non-Qualified (Ordinary) Dividends:**
- Taxed at ordinary income rates (10%-37%)
- REITs, MLPs, some preferred stocks

**Tax-Efficient Fund Selection (Taxable Accounts):**

**Efficient:**
- ✅ Index funds (low turnover)
- ✅ ETFs (in-kind redemptions avoid capital gains)
- ✅ Tax-managed funds
- ✅ Individual stocks (control timing of sales)
- ✅ Municipal bonds (tax-exempt interest)

**Inefficient:**
- ❌ Actively managed funds (high turnover)
- ❌ REIT funds (non-qualified dividends)
- ❌ Bond funds (ordinary income)
- ❌ High-dividend stocks (if in high bracket)

**Asset Location for Tax Efficiency:**
- **Taxable account:** Index funds, individual stocks, muni bonds
- **Tax-deferred:** Bonds, REITs, actively managed funds
- **Roth:** Highest growth potential (small-cap, emerging markets)

## Charitable Giving Strategies

### Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs)

**Rules:**
- Age 70½ or older
- Direct transfer from IRA to 501(c)(3) charity
- Up to $105,000 per year (2024, indexed)
- Counts toward RMD
- Not included in taxable income

**Benefits Over Cash Donation:**
- Reduces AGI (vs itemized deduction)
- Works even if take standard deduction
- Reduces Medicare IRMAA
- Reduces Social Security taxation
- Keeps income lower for other means-tested benefits

**Example:**
```
Age 75, RMD $60,000, want to donate $25,000 to charity

Option 1: Take RMD, donate cash
- RMD: $60,000 (taxable)
- Donation: $25,000 (itemized deduction, if exceeds standard deduction)
- AGI: $60,000
- If can't itemize or use standard deduction, no tax benefit from donation

Option 2: QCD
- QCD $25,000 to charity (satisfies part of RMD)
- Take remaining $35,000 RMD
- AGI: $35,000
- **$25,000 lower AGI**

Tax Savings (22% bracket): $25,000 × 22% = $5,500
Plus: Lower AGI helps with IRMAA, SS taxation, other benefits
```

### Donor-Advised Funds (DAFs)

**What is DAF:**
- Charitable account at sponsoring organization (Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, etc.)
- Immediate tax deduction when contribute
- Funds grow tax-free
- Recommend grants to charities over time

**Benefits:**
- Deduct now, grant later (timing flexibility)
- Invest contributions, potential growth
- Simplify recordkeeping
- Donate appreciated securities (avoid capital gains tax)
- "Bunch" multiple years of donations into one year for larger deduction

**Bunching Strategy:**

**Problem:** Standard deduction is high ($30,000 MFJ), hard to exceed with itemizing

**Solution:** Bunch 3-5 years of donations into DAF in one year

**Example:**
```
Normal annual giving: $15,000/year to various charities
Standard deduction: $30,000 (MFJ)

Traditional approach:
- Donate $15,000 each year
- Take standard deduction (donation doesn't reduce tax)
- No tax benefit from charitable giving

Bunching with DAF:
Year 1:
- Contribute $75,000 to DAF (5 years × $15,000)
- Itemized deductions: $75,000 (charity) + $10,000 (SALT) + $8,000 (mortgage interest) = $93,000
- Itemize (exceeds $30,000 standard deduction)
- Extra deduction: $93,000 - $30,000 = $63,000
- Tax savings (24% bracket): $63,000 × 24% = $15,120

Years 2-5:
- Take standard deduction: $30,000/year
- Grant $15,000/year from DAF to charities (no additional deduction, already took it)

Result: $15,120 tax savings vs $0 with annual donations
```

### Donating Appreciated Securities

**Strategy:** Donate appreciated stock instead of cash

**Benefits:**
- Avoid capital gains tax on appreciation
- Deduct full fair market value (if held >1 year)
- Charity receives full value (no tax)

**Example:**
```
Want to donate $10,000 to charity

Option 1: Donate cash
- Write $10,000 check
- Itemized deduction: $10,000
- Tax savings (24% bracket): $2,400

Option 2: Donate appreciated stock
- Stock cost basis: $3,000
- Current value: $10,000
- Appreciation: $7,000

Process:
- Transfer stock to charity
- Itemized deduction: $10,000 (FMV)
- Avoided capital gains tax: $7,000 × 15% = $1,050

Total Benefit:
- Deduction tax savings: $10,000 × 24% = $2,400
- Capital gains avoided: $1,050
- **Total tax benefit: $3,450** (vs $2,400 cash donation)
```

**Donate Stock to DAF, Then Grant Cash to Charities:**
- Maximize tax efficiency (avoid cap gains, get deduction)
- Charities receive cash grants from DAF

### Charitable Remainder Trusts (CRTs)

**What is CRT:**
- Irrevocable trust
- Donate assets to trust
- Receive income for period of years or life
- Remainder goes to charity

**Types:**

**CRUT (Charitable Remainder Unitrust):**
- Income is % of trust value (revalued annually)
- If trust grows, income increases

**CRAT (Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust):**
- Fixed income amount each year
- Doesn't change with trust value

**Benefits:**
- Immediate partial tax deduction (present value of remainder to charity)
- Avoid capital gains on appreciated assets contributed
- Income stream for years/life
- Reduces estate

**Example:**
```
Age 65, highly appreciated stock (basis $100,000, value $1,000,000)
Want income but donating to charity eventually

CRUT Structure:
- Contribute $1,000,000 stock to CRUT
- Receive 5% annual income for life ($50,000/year initially)
- At death, remainder to charity

Tax Benefits:
- Avoid $900,000 capital gain (save ~$180,000 in cap gains tax)
- Immediate charitable deduction: ~$400,000 (present value of remainder)
- Deduction savings (37% bracket): $148,000
- Income for life: $50,000+/year (grows if trust grows)

Total Tax Benefit: $180,000 (cap gains) + $148,000 (deduction) = $328,000
```

**Considerations:**
- Irrevocable (can't change mind)
- Complex and expensive to set up
- Best for large donations ($500,000+)

## Estate and Gift Tax Planning

### Federal Estate and Gift Tax Exemption

**2025 Exemption:** $13,990,000 per person ($27,980,000 married couple)

**Scheduled Sunset:** Exemption drops to ~$7 million (indexed) on January 1, 2026 unless extended

**Tax Rate on Amount Over Exemption:** 40%

### Annual Gift Tax Exclusion

**2025:** $19,000 per recipient per year ($38,000 if married couple gives jointly)

**Unlimited Exclusions:**
- Payments directly to medical providers
- Payments directly to educational institutions (tuition only, not room/board)
- Gifts to spouse (if U.S. citizen)
- Gifts to charities

**Example:**
```
Married couple, 3 adult children, 6 grandchildren

Annual Gifting (2025):
- Each child: $38,000 × 3 = $114,000
- Each grandchild: $38,000 × 6 = $228,000
- Total annual gifting: $342,000 (no gift tax, no estate tax impact)

10-Year Gifting: $3,420,000 removed from estate, tax-free
```

### Estate Tax Reduction Strategies

**For Estates Over $13.99M (single) or $27.98M (married):**

**1. Lifetime Gifting (Use Exemption Now)**
- Current exemption: $13.99M
- Sunsets to ~$7M in 2026
- Gift now to use higher exemption before sunset
- Future appreciation also out of estate

**Example:**
```
Estate value: $20 million, expected to grow to $35 million

Strategy: Gift $13.99M to irrevocable trust in 2025
- Uses full current exemption
- $13.99M + growth out of estate
- If gifted assets grow to $20M by death, that $20M is out of estate
- Estate at death: $35M - $20M = $15M (under married exemption)
- Estate tax: $0

Without gifting:
- Estate at death: $35M
- Exemption (2026 sunset): $14M
- Taxable: $21M
- Estate tax: $21M × 40% = $8.4M
```

**2. Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT)**
- Irrevocable trust for spouse's benefit
- Use lifetime exemption to fund
- Spouse can receive income/principal
- Removes assets from both estates
- Divorce or spouse's death can be issues

**3. Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)**
- Transfer assets to trust
- Receive annuity for term of years
- Remainder to beneficiaries gift-tax-free if assets outperform IRS rate
- "Zeroed-out" GRAT minimizes gift tax

**4. Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)**
- Life insurance owned by irrevocable trust
- Death benefit not in estate
- Provides liquidity for estate taxes if needed
- Trust owns policy, pays premiums

### Portability of Estate Tax Exemption

**Portability:** Surviving spouse can use deceased spouse's unused exemption

**Requirements:**
- Must file estate tax return (Form 706) within 9 months of death (even if no tax owed)
- Elects portability on return

**Example:**
```
Husband dies in 2025, estate $5 million
Exemption: $13.99 million
Unused: $8.99 million

Wife files Form 706, elects portability
Wife's exemption: $13.99M (own) + $8.99M (husband's unused) = $22.98M

Wife's estate can be up to $22.98M with no estate tax
```

**Note:** Portability is "last deceased spouse" only. If remarry and new spouse dies, lose first spouse's exemption.

### Step-Up in Basis at Death

**Rule:** Assets in estate receive step-up in basis to fair market value at date of death

**Benefit:** Eliminates capital gains tax on appreciation during lifetime

**Example:**
```
Stock purchased in 1980: $10,000
Value at death (2025): $1,000,000
Unrealized gain: $990,000

Without death:
- If sold during life: $990,000 × 20% LTCG = $198,000 tax

With death (step-up):
- Heirs inherit at $1,000,000 basis
- Immediately sell for $1,000,000
- Capital gain: $0
- Tax: $0
- **Saved $198,000 in capital gains tax**
```

**Planning Implications:**

**Hold Low-Basis Assets Until Death:**
- Avoid realizing gains during life if large estate
- Let heirs receive step-up

**Roth Conversion Tradeoff:**
- Roth conversion realizes income, pays tax
- Traditional IRA gets no step-up (always taxed as ordinary income to heirs)
- Roth IRA inherited tax-free
- If large estate, may be better to pay tax via Roth conversion than leave Traditional IRA

### Beneficiary Designations

**Assets That Pass by Beneficiary Designation (outside of will/probate):**
- Retirement accounts (IRA, 401k)
- Life insurance
- Annuities
- Transfer on Death (TOD) / Payable on Death (POD) accounts

**Common Mistakes:**
- Outdated beneficiaries (ex-spouse, deceased person)
- No contingent beneficiary
- Naming minor children directly (requires court-appointed guardian)
- Naming estate as beneficiary (loses stretch, goes through probate)

**Best Practices:**
- Review beneficiaries annually
- Name primary and contingent beneficiaries
- Consider trust as beneficiary if minor children or special needs
- Ensure coordination with estate plan

**Spousal Rollover (IRA):**
- Surviving spouse can rollover inherited IRA to own IRA
- Treats as own (own RMD schedule, can delay to 73/75)
- Can name own beneficiaries
- Almost always best option for spouse

**Non-Spouse Beneficiary (Inherited IRA):**
- 10-year rule (must distribute within 10 years)
- Plan distributions to minimize tax impact
- No spousal rollover option

## Business Owner Tax Strategies

### Business Entity Tax Treatment

**Sole Proprietorship / Single-Member LLC:**
- Pass-through taxation (Schedule C)
- Self-employment tax on all net income (15.3%)
- No separate entity tax return

**Partnership / Multi-Member LLC:**
- Pass-through taxation (Form 1065)
- Partners pay tax on distributive share (K-1)
- Self-employment tax on active partner income

**S Corporation:**
- Pass-through taxation (Form 1120-S)
- Reasonable salary subject to payroll tax
- Distributions not subject to self-employment tax
- Limited to 100 shareholders, one class of stock

**C Corporation:**
- Separate tax entity (Form 1120)
- Corporate tax: 21% flat rate
- Dividends to shareholders taxed again ("double taxation")
- Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) exclusion potential

### S Corp vs LLC for Self-Employed

**LLC (taxed as sole proprietorship):**
```
Net business income: $200,000
Self-employment tax: $200,000 × 92.35% × 15.3% = $28,278
Income tax (24% bracket): $200,000 × 24% = $48,000
Total tax: $76,278
```

**S Corp:**
```
Reasonable salary: $100,000 (W-2)
Distribution: $100,000 (not subject to SE tax)

Payroll tax on salary:
- Social Security/Medicare: $100,000 × 7.65% (employee) = $7,650
- Employer portion: $100,000 × 7.65% = $7,650
- Total payroll tax: $15,300

Income tax: $200,000 × 24% = $48,000

Total tax: $63,300

Savings: $76,278 - $63,300 = $12,978
```

**S Corp Considerations:**
- Must pay "reasonable salary" (IRS scrutiny if too low)
- Payroll compliance (quarterly 941, W-2, etc.)
- Additional costs (payroll service, accounting)
- Worth it if saving >$5,000-10,000/year

### Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction

**Section 199A:** 20% deduction on qualified business income for pass-through entities

**Phase-Out (2025):**
- Begins: $197,300 single, $394,600 MFJ
- Fully phased out: $247,300 single, $494,600 MFJ

**Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB):**
- Doctors, lawyers, accountants, consultants, financial advisors, etc.
- QBI deduction phases out in phase-out range

**Example (Non-SSTB):**
```
S Corp, pass-through income $150,000 (under threshold)

QBI Deduction: $150,000 × 20% = $30,000

Taxable income:
- Gross income: $150,000
- QBI deduction: -$30,000
- Taxable income: $120,000 (for income tax calculation)

Effective tax rate reduction: 20% × marginal rate
- If in 24% bracket: 24% × 20% = 4.8% effective rate reduction
```

**Example (SSTB above threshold):**
```
Financial advisor, income $300,000 (single)
Fully phased out (over $247,300)

QBI deduction: $0 (phased out for SSTB)
```

### Solo 401(k) / SEP IRA for Self-Employed

**Solo 401(k) Contributions (2025):**
```
Employee deferrals: $23,500 ($31,000 if age 50+)
Employer profit-sharing: 25% of compensation (20% of SE income after SE tax deduction)
Total limit: $70,000 ($77,500 if age 50+)
```

**Example:**
```
Self-employed income: $200,000
Self-employment tax deduction: ~$14,130
Net SE income: $185,870

Solo 401(k) Contributions:
- Employee deferral: $23,500 (age <50)
- Employer: $185,870 × 20% = $37,174
- Total: $60,674

Tax savings (24% bracket): $60,674 × 24% = $14,562
```

**SEP IRA:**
- Simpler than Solo 401(k)
- Up to 25% of compensation (20% of SE income)
- No employee deferral component
- Max: $70,000 (2025)

### Hiring Family Members

**Hire Children:**
- Legitimate work for business
- Pay reasonable wages
- W-2 or 1099 (if 1099, child pays SE tax)
- Shifts income from parent's high bracket to child's low bracket

**Benefits:**
- Child's standard deduction: $15,000 (2025) shields wages from tax
- Funds child's Roth IRA (up to $7,000 or earned income, whichever less)
- Reduces parent's taxable income

**Example:**
```
Parent in 32% tax bracket
Hire child (age 16) for legitimate work: $12,000/year

Parent's tax savings: $12,000 × 32% = $3,840
Child's tax: $0 (under standard deduction)

Child contributes $7,000 to Roth IRA:
- $7,000 at age 16, growing at 7% until age 65 (49 years)
- Roth value: $235,000 (tax-free in retirement)
```

**Requirements:**
- Work must be legitimate and age-appropriate
- Compensation must be reasonable
- Keep time records, job descriptions

## State Tax Considerations

### High-Tax vs Low-Tax States

**High-Tax States (9%+ top rate):**
- California (13.3% top rate)
- Hawaii (11%)
- New York (10.9%)
- New Jersey (10.75%)
- Oregon (9.9%)

**No Income Tax States:**
- Alaska, Florida, Nevada, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wyoming

**Residency Planning:**
- Establish residency in low/no-tax state before retirement
- Avoid domicile issues (IRS and states can challenge)
- Consider: Days present, driver's license, voter registration, home location

**Example Tax Savings:**
```
$200,000 retirement income

California resident:
- Federal tax: ~$32,000 (estimated)
- California state tax: ~$14,000 (7% effective rate)
- Total: $46,000

Florida resident (no state tax):
- Federal tax: ~$32,000
- State tax: $0
- Total: $32,000

Annual savings: $14,000
30-year retirement savings: $420,000+
```

### SALT Deduction Limitation

**State and Local Tax (SALT) Deduction Cap:** $10,000 (since 2018 TCJA)

**Includes:**
- State income tax (or state sales tax if elected)
- Property tax
- Total capped at $10,000

**Scheduled to Expire:** December 31, 2025 (reverts to unlimited unless extended)

**Workarounds (for business owners):**
- Pass-through Entity Tax (PTET) elections in many states
- Business pays state tax at entity level
- Owner deducts on federal return as business expense (not SALT, avoids $10,000 cap)
- Effectively converts limited SALT deduction to unlimited business expense
