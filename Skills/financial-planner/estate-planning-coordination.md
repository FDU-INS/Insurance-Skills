# Estate Planning Coordination for CFP® Professionals

Guide to coordinating estate planning as part of comprehensive financial planning.

## CFP® Professional's Role

**Scope of Practice:**
- Identify estate planning needs
- Recommend estate planning strategies
- Coordinate with estate planning attorney
- Ensure beneficiary designations align with estate plan
- Review titling of assets
- Monitor and update as circumstances change

**Outside Scope (refer to attorney):**
- Drafting wills, trusts, powers of attorney
- Providing legal advice
- Practicing law

## Essential Estate Planning Documents

### Last Will and Testament

**Purpose:** Directs distribution of probate assets at death

**Key Provisions:**
- Executor/Personal Representative designation
- Beneficiaries and bequests
- Guardian for minor children
- Specific bequests (personal property, heirlooms)
- Residuary clause (catch-all for remaining assets)

**Assets Controlled by Will:**
- Assets titled in individual name only
- No beneficiary designation
- Not in trust

**Assets NOT Controlled by Will (pass outside probate):**
- Joint tenancy with right of survivorship
- Beneficiary designations (IRA, 401k, life insurance)
- Payable on Death / Transfer on Death accounts
- Trust assets

### Revocable Living Trust

**Purpose:** Avoid probate, provide management if incapacitated, privacy

**Key Terms:**
- Grantor/Settlor: Person creating trust (you)
- Trustee: Person managing trust (you during life, successor after)
- Beneficiaries: Recipients of trust assets

**Advantages:**
- Avoids probate (faster, less expensive, private)
- Continues if incapacitated (no conservatorship needed)
- Can manage assets across multiple states
- Privacy (not public record like will)

**Disadvantages:**
- Cost to create ($2,000-$5,000+)
- Must re-title assets to trust
- Ongoing administration

**When to Recommend:**
- Estate value >$500,000
- Own real estate in multiple states
- Privacy concerns
- Want to avoid probate

### Powers of Attorney

**Durable Power of Attorney for Finances:**
- Authorizes agent to manage financial affairs if incapacitated
- "Durable" means survives incapacity
- Terminates at death

**Healthcare Power of Attorney:**
- Authorizes agent to make medical decisions if incapacitated
- HIPAA authorization to access medical records
- Separate from financial POA

**Living Will / Advance Directive:**
- States end-of-life care preferences
- Life support, artificial nutrition/hydration
- Guides healthcare agent's decisions

**Importance:**
Without POAs, family must petition court for conservatorship/guardianship (expensive, time-consuming, public)

## Asset Titling Strategies

### Types of Ownership

**Individual / Sole Ownership:**
- Controlled by will
- Goes through probate
- Subject to estate tax (if over exemption)

**Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship (JTWROS):**
- Passes automatically to surviving owner
- Avoids probate
- Can create unintended consequences (survivor owns 100%)

**Tenants in Common:**
- Each owner's share passes via their will
- Goes through probate
- Allows unequal ownership percentages

**Community Property (9 states):**
- AZ, CA, ID, LA, NV, NM, TX, WA, WI
- Each spouse owns 50%
- Double step-up in basis at first death (entire property, not just deceased's half)

### Primary Residence Titling

**Married Couple Options:**

**Joint Tenants with Right of Survivorship:**
- Most common
- Surviving spouse gets full ownership automatically
- One step-up in basis (only deceased spouse's half)

**Community Property (if in CP state):**
- Preferable to JTWROS in CP states
- Double step-up in basis at first death
- Saves capital gains tax for survivor

**Revocable Living Trust:**
- Avoids probate
- Allows planning for incapacity
- Can include terms for what happens at first vs second death

**Example - Basis Step-Up Difference:**
```
Home purchased 1980: $100,000
Value at husband's death 2025: $900,000
Appreciation: $800,000

JTWROS (non-CP state):
- Husband's half steps up: $450,000 new basis
- Wife's half remains: $50,000 old basis
- New total basis: $500,000

If wife sells for $900,000:
- Gain: $900,000 - $500,000 = $400,000
- Primary residence exclusion: -$250,000 (single)
- Taxable gain: $150,000
- Tax (20% LTCG): $30,000

Community Property (CP state):
- Entire property steps up to $900,000

If wife sells for $900,000:
- Gain: $900,000 - $900,000 = $0
- Tax: $0
- **Saved: $30,000**
```

### Retirement Account Beneficiaries

**Primary Beneficiary Options:**

**Spouse:**
- Can roll over to own IRA (best option usually)
- Treats as own, delays RMDs to own age 73/75
- Can name new beneficiaries
- Can disclaim to contingent beneficiary

**Non-Spouse Individual:**
- 10-year distribution rule
- Must empty account within 10 years
- Plan distributions to minimize tax

**Trust:**
- Provides control and protection
- Consider if minor children, special needs, or creditor protection needed
- More complex, ensure "see-through" trust rules met

**Charity:**
- If charitably inclined and have other assets for heirs
- Charity pays no income tax
- Heirs inherit other assets with step-up in basis (better tax treatment)

**Estate:**
- Worst option (avoid)
- Loses "stretch" treatment
- Must distribute quickly
- Goes through probate

**Contingent Beneficiary:**
- Backup if primary predeceases
- Avoid minor children as direct beneficiaries (court-appointed guardian required)

### Life Insurance Beneficiaries

**Primary: Spouse (typical)**
- Tax-free death benefit
- Provides liquidity

**Contingent: Children**
- If spouse predeceases
- Consider trust if minor children

**Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT):**
- Removes death benefit from estate (estate tax planning)
- Trust owns policy, receives death benefit
- Provides liquidity for estate taxes
- Beneficiaries receive distributions from trust

## Trust Strategies for Specific Situations

### Special Needs Trust

**Purpose:** Provide for disabled beneficiary without disqualifying from government benefits

**Structure:**
- Third-party trust funded by parent/grandparent
- Trustee has discretion for distributions
- Does not count as beneficiary's asset for SSI/Medicaid

**Importance:**
- Direct inheritance would disqualify from benefits
- Trust preserves eligibility while supplementing needs

### Spendthrift Trust

**Purpose:** Protect assets from beneficiary's creditors and poor financial decisions

**Provisions:**
- Beneficiary cannot transfer interest
- Creditors cannot reach trust assets
- Trustee controls distributions

**When to Consider:**
- Beneficiary with substance abuse issues
- Beneficiary in high-liability profession
- Beneficiary with poor money management skills
- Protect from divorce (beneficiary's spouse cannot claim)

### Qualified Terminable Interest Property (QTIP) Trust

**Purpose:** Provide for surviving spouse while controlling ultimate distribution to children

**Structure:**
- Surviving spouse receives income for life
- At spouse's death, remainder to designated beneficiaries (e.g., children from first marriage)

**Use Cases:**
- Blended families
- Ensure children from first marriage inherit
- Spouse gets use during life, children get remainder

**Tax Treatment:**
- Qualifies for unlimited marital deduction
- Included in surviving spouse's estate

### Bypass Trust (Credit Shelter Trust)

**Purpose:** Use both spouses' estate tax exemptions

**Less Relevant Now:**
- With portability, surviving spouse can use deceased spouse's unused exemption
- Less need for bypass trusts

**Still Useful For:**
- State estate tax planning (portability may not apply to state)
- Creditor protection for surviving spouse
- Remarriage concerns

### Generation-Skipping Transfer (GST) Trust

**Purpose:** Transfer wealth to grandchildren, skipping children's generation

**Benefits:**
- Avoids estate tax at children's generation
- Leverages GST tax exemption ($13.99M in 2025)

**Use Case:**
- Children financially secure, prefer wealth go to grandchildren
- Maximize multi-generational wealth transfer

## Charitable Planning Integration

### Charitable Bequests in Will

**Simple:** "I give $50,000 to [charity name]"

**Residuary:** "I give 10% of my remaining estate to [charity]"

**Advantage:** Easy, no cost during life

**Disadvantage:** Heirs pay tax on retirement accounts, charity gets cash (inefficient)

### Retirement Account to Charity

**Strategy:** Name charity as beneficiary of IRA

**Advantages:**
- IRA never taxed (charity tax-exempt)
- Other assets to heirs with step-up in basis
- Heirs inherit assets with better tax treatment

**Example:**
```
Estate: $3M
- IRA: $1M
- Taxable brokerage: $1M (basis $200,000)
- Home: $1M (basis $300,000)

Charitable Intent: $1M to charity

Option 1: Leave cash/securities to charity
- Charity gets $1M from brokerage/home
- Heirs get $1M IRA (fully taxable as ordinary income)
- Heirs pay ~$300,000+ income tax on inherited IRA

Option 2: Leave IRA to charity
- Charity gets $1M IRA (pays no tax)
- Heirs get $2M taxable brokerage + home (step-up in basis to $2M)
- Heirs pay $0 tax on inherited assets (step-up eliminates gain)
- **Tax savings for heirs: $300,000+**
```

## Estate Tax Planning

### Current Law (2025)

**Exemption:** $13,990,000 per person ($27,980,000 married)

**Sunset Provision:** Drops to ~$7M (indexed) on January 1, 2026 unless extended

**Tax Rate:** 40% on amount above exemption

### Strategies for Estates Over Exemption

**1. Lifetime Gifting**
- Annual exclusion: $19,000 per recipient (2025)
- Lifetime gifts reduce taxable estate
- Use high exemption before 2026 sunset

**2. Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT)**
- Irrevocable trust for spouse
- Removes from estate, but spouse can benefit
- Use lifetime exemption now

**3. Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)**
- Trust owns life insurance policy
- Death benefit not in estate
- Provides liquidity for estate tax

**4. Family Limited Partnership (FLP)**
- Transfer business/real estate to FLP
- Gift limited partnership interests to children
- Valuation discounts (lack of control, marketability)

**5. Charitable Planning**
- Charitable gifts reduce estate
- CRT, CLT, donor-advised fund, direct bequests

## Beneficiary Designation Coordination

### Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Outdated Beneficiaries**
- Ex-spouse still named
- Deceased person listed
- Minor children named directly

**Solution:** Annual review, update after life events

**Mistake 2: No Contingent Beneficiary**
- Primary beneficiary predeceases, goes to estate
- Loses intended distribution plan

**Solution:** Always name contingent beneficiaries

**Mistake 3: Beneficiaries Conflict with Estate Plan**
- Will says split 3 ways, but IRA names only one child
- Trust carefully drafted, but assets pass by beneficiary designation

**Solution:** Coordinate all beneficiary designations with overall estate plan

**Mistake 4: Retirement Account to Estate**
- Loses stretch treatment
- Accelerated taxation
- Goes through probate

**Solution:** Name individuals or see-through trust

### Optimal Beneficiary Structure (Married, 2 Children)

**Retirement Accounts (IRA, 401k):**
- Primary: Spouse
- Contingent: Children in equal shares (or trust if minors)

**Life Insurance:**
- Primary: Spouse
- Contingent: Children in equal shares (or trust if minors)
- Or: ILIT as owner/beneficiary (estate tax planning)

**Taxable Brokerage (TOD):**
- Primary: Spouse
- Contingent: Children in equal shares

**Home:**
- Titled: Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship (or in trust)
- Passes automatically to spouse

**Revocable Living Trust:**
- Beneficiaries: Spouse first, then children
- Terms in trust document control distribution

## Special Situations

### Blended Families

**Challenge:** Balancing current spouse's needs with desire to provide for children from prior marriage

**Solutions:**

**QTIP Trust:**
- Current spouse receives income for life
- Children from first marriage receive remainder at spouse's death

**Life Insurance:**
- Policy payable to children from first marriage
- Current spouse provided for via other assets

**Prenuptial Agreement:**
- Define what goes to whom
- Waive spousal rights to certain assets

### Minor Children

**Guardianship:** Name in will

**Financial Provision:**
- Do not name minor children as direct beneficiaries (court-appointed guardian required)
- Name trust as beneficiary
- Trust terms control distributions (age milestones, education, health, support)

**Example Trust Terms:**
```
Ages 18-25: Distributions for health, education, maintenance, support (HEMS)
Age 25: 1/3 of principal outright
Age 30: 1/2 of remaining principal outright
Age 35: Final distribution, terminate trust
```

### Business Owners

**Succession Planning:**
- Who will inherit/run business?
- Buy-sell agreement funded with life insurance
- Valuation method

**Estate Tax Liquidity:**
- Business may be illiquid (real estate, private company)
- Life insurance provides liquidity to pay estate tax without forced sale

**Gifting Business Interests:**
- Transfer to children during life using annual exclusion and lifetime exemption
- Valuation discounts (minority interest, lack of marketability)
- Freezes value at current level, future growth out of estate

## CFP® Professional Action Items

### Initial Estate Planning Review

1. **Inventory Existing Documents:**
   - Will, trusts, powers of attorney
   - Review provisions, age of documents, attorney used

2. **Review Beneficiary Designations:**
   - Retirement accounts
   - Life insurance
   - Payable/Transfer on Death accounts
   - Ensure consistency with estate plan

3. **Asset Titling Review:**
   - How is each asset titled?
   - Does titling align with estate plan goals?
   - Recommendations for changes

4. **Identify Gaps:**
   - Missing documents (no will, no POA)
   - Outdated provisions
   - Inconsistencies

5. **Recommend Strategy:**
   - Refer to estate planning attorney
   - Coordinate implementation
   - Follow up to ensure completion

### Ongoing Monitoring (Annual Review)

- Have there been life changes? (Marriage, divorce, birth, death)
- Are beneficiaries still current?
- Has tax law changed?
- Are estate documents still appropriate?

### Life Event Triggers (Immediate Review)

- Marriage or divorce
- Birth or adoption
- Death of spouse or beneficiary
- Significant wealth change
- Move to different state
- Tax law change affecting estate planning
