# Trust Planning for Protection Policies

## Why Use Trusts for Protection?

### 1. Avoid Inheritance Tax (IHT)

**Without Trust**:
- Life insurance payout forms part of estate
- Subject to 40% IHT if estate over nil rate band
- Example: £300k policy + £400k estate = £700k total
- IHT on £325k (over NRB): £150,000 tax

**With Trust**:
- Payout outside estate
- No IHT on life insurance
- Example: £300k in trust + £400k estate
- Only £400k counts, tax on £75k = £30,000
- **Saving: £120,000**

### 2. Faster Payment

**Without Trust**:
- Wait for probate (4-12 months)
- Grant of representation required
- Delays during difficult time

**With Trust**:
- Trustees claim directly
- Payment in 2-4 weeks (usually)
- No probate needed for policy

### 3. Control Over Beneficiaries

**Without Trust**:
- Paid to estate
- Distributed per will (or intestacy rules)
- Spouse gets everything (if married)
- Children from first marriage may get nothing

**With Trust**:
- Trustees decide distribution
- Can protect children's inheritance
- Flexibility for circumstances
- Can prevent wasteful spending

### 4. Protection from Creditors

**Without Trust**:
- Forms part of estate
- Available to creditors
- Vulnerable to bankruptcy proceedings

**With Trust**:
- Outside estate (if properly structured)
- Protected from creditors (usually)
- Safeguards family's financial security

### 5. Flexibility

**Discretionary Trust**:
- Trustees have discretion
- Can adapt to changing circumstances
- Not fixed at outset

## Types of Protection Trusts

### 1. Discretionary Trust (Most Common)

**How It Works**:
- Trustees hold benefit
- Discretion over who receives what and when
- Can distribute to any beneficiary
- Flexible approach

**Beneficiaries**:
- Named class of people (e.g., "spouse and children")
- Trustees choose who gets what
- Can add/exclude beneficiaries (within class)

**Example**:
- Beneficiaries: Spouse and children
- Payout: £300,000
- Trustees decide:
  - Spouse: £150,000 (immediate)
  - Children: £75,000 each (held until age 25)

**Advantages**:
- Maximum flexibility
- Adapts to circumstances
- Trustees can consider needs
- IHT-efficient

**Disadvantages**:
- Less certainty for beneficiaries
- Potential for disputes
- Trustees must make decisions

**Best For**:
- Complex families (second marriages, step-children)
- Young children (protect until mature)
- Uncertain future circumstances
- Maximum IHT efficiency

### 2. Absolute Trust

**How It Works**:
- Fixed beneficiaries named
- No discretion
- Beneficiaries have immediate entitlement
- Cannot change later

**Example**:
- Beneficiary: Spouse (100%)
- Payout: £300,000
- Spouse receives: £300,000 (automatic)
- No trustee discretion

**Advantages**:
- Certainty for beneficiaries
- Simple and clear
- Immediate entitlement
- No ongoing trust administration

**Disadvantages**:
- No flexibility
- Cannot adapt to changes
- If beneficiary dies before you, trust fails
- Less IHT-efficient (gift with reservation potential)

**Best For**:
- Simple families
- Certainty preferred
- Adult beneficiaries
- Stable circumstances

### 3. Split Trust

**How It Works**:
- Different benefits to different beneficiaries
- Life insurance to one, critical illness to another
- Fixed allocation

**Example**:
- Life insurance (death): To spouse
- Critical illness (diagnosis): To policyholder
- Allows claiming CI without giving money away

**Advantages**:
- Separates death and CI benefits
- Keeps CI benefit for policyholder
- Spouse protected on death

**Disadvantages**:
- Less common
- More complex
- Limited flexibility
- Not all providers offer

**Best For**:
- Combined life and CI policies
- Want to keep CI benefit personally
- Spouse protected on death

### 4. Survivor's Discretionary Trust

**How It Works**:
- For joint life first death policies
- Survivor becomes trustee
- Survivor has discretion over distribution
- Children as beneficiaries

**Example**:
- Joint life policy: £250,000
- Husband dies
- Wife becomes trustee
- Can distribute to self and children as needed

**Advantages**:
- Survivor has control
- Protects children's interests
- IHT-efficient
- Flexibility for survivor

**Disadvantages**:
- Only for joint policies
- Survivor must act as trustee
- Less protection if survivor remarries

**Best For**:
- Joint life policies
- Trust survivor to distribute fairly
- Want survivor in control
- Young families

### 5. Loan Trust (Advanced)

**How It Works**:
- Loan to trustees
- Trustees invest in bond/policy
- Growth outside estate
- Loan repaid from proceeds

**Complex**: Specialist advice needed

**Use**: Wealthy clients, investment bonds, IHT planning

**Not Common**: For straightforward protection

### 6. Gift Trust

**How It Works**:
- Gift premiums into trust
- Trustees own policy
- Potentially exempt transfer (7-year rule)

**IHT Planning**:
- Premiums are gifts
- If survive 7 years, outside estate
- If die within 7 years, taper relief

**Use**: Regular premium policies, IHT planning

## Trust Administration

### Setting Up Trust

**Process**:
1. Complete trust deed
2. Sign and witness (policyholder and trustee)
3. Send to insurance provider
4. Provider notes trust on policy
5. Keep copy of trust deed safe

**Timing**:
- Ideally at policy inception
- Can add trust later (usually)
- Some providers charge for later trusts

**Cost**:
- Usually free (provider supplies template)
- Solicitor trust: £300-£1,000 (rarely needed)

### Trustees

**Who Can Be Trustee**:
- Anyone over 18
- Usually 2-4 trustees
- Commonly: Spouse, adult children, friends, solicitor

**Role of Trustees**:
- Hold policy on behalf of beneficiaries
- Make claim on death/diagnosis
- Distribute proceeds to beneficiaries
- Make decisions (discretionary trust)
- Act in beneficiaries' best interests

**Number of Trustees**:
- Minimum: 2 (recommended)
- Maximum: 4 (typical)
- Sole trustee: Possible but not recommended

**Choosing Trustees**:
- Trustworthy and responsible
- Understand beneficiaries' needs
- Available and willing
- Ideally 1-2 trustees younger than you
- Consider professional trustee (solicitor) for complex situations

**Trustee Powers**:
- Claim policy proceeds
- Invest proceeds (if not immediately distributed)
- Decide distribution (discretionary trust)
- Add/remove beneficiaries (within class)
- Appoint new trustees

### Beneficiaries

**Discretionary Trust**:
- Named class (e.g., "spouse, children, grandchildren")
- Not fixed individuals
- Flexible

**Absolute Trust**:
- Named individuals
- Fixed
- Cannot change

**Common Beneficiary Classes**:
- "Spouse and children"
- "Children only"
- "Spouse, children, and grandchildren"
- "Any relative"

**Excluding Beneficiaries**:
- Can specifically exclude (e.g., estranged child)
- Document reasons (letter of wishes)

### Letter of Wishes

**What It Is**: Non-binding guidance to trustees

**Includes**:
- How you'd like proceeds distributed
- Reasons for decisions
- Special circumstances
- Future considerations

**Example**:
> "I wish the trustees to ensure my spouse has sufficient funds for living expenses and mortgage. If there are surplus funds after my spouse's needs are met, I would like my children to receive equal shares, held in trust until age 25 for education and first home deposit."

**Status**:
- Not legally binding
- Guidance only
- Trustees can deviate if circumstances warrant

**Update**:
- Review every 3-5 years
- After major life events
- Keep with trust deed

## Trust Tax Treatment

### Income Tax

**Trust Income**:
- Dividends: 39.35% (trust rate)
- Interest: 20% (trust rate)

**Distributed to Beneficiaries**:
- Beneficiary pays tax at their rate
- Credit for tax paid by trust

**Usually**: Proceeds paid out quickly, minimal income

### Capital Gains Tax

**Trust Gains**:
- CGT rate: 24% (trust rate)
- Annual exemption: £1,500 (trust)

**Usually**: Proceeds paid in cash, no CGT

### Inheritance Tax

**Discretionary Trust**:
- 10-year charge: Usually nil (if below nil rate band)
- Exit charge: Usually nil
- **If policy proceeds <£325,000**: No IHT

**Absolute Trust**:
- Potentially exempt transfer (PET)
- If die within 7 years of creating trust: May be IHT
- Unlikely issue for protection policies

**Ongoing Premiums**:
- Gifts (if discretionary trust)
- Usually covered by £3,000 annual exemption
- Or normal expenditure exemption

**Practical Impact**:
- Most protection policies: No IHT issues
- Trust keeps proceeds outside estate
- Achieves IHT objective

## Changing Trusts

### Changing Trustees

**Reasons**:
- Trustee dies
- Trustee no longer suitable
- Want to add trustee

**Process**:
- Deed of appointment (or retirement)
- Notify insurer
- Update records

**When**:
- Review trustee appointments every 5 years
- After major life changes

### Changing Beneficiaries

**Discretionary Trust**:
- Trustees can add/remove (within class)
- Deed of appointment
- Subject to trust deed terms

**Absolute Trust**:
- Cannot change
- Fixed at creation

### Revoking Trust

**When Possible**:
- Depends on trust type and deed
- Usually not possible (absolute)
- Sometimes possible (discretionary, with consent)

**Why You Might Want To**:
- Circumstances changed
- Want to change trust type
- Made error

**Process**:
- Legal advice essential
- All trustees and beneficiaries consent
- Deed of revocation

**Better**: Get it right first time

## Common Trust Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Young Family

**Situation**:
- Married, 2 young children (ages 3 and 5)
- £300,000 life insurance

**Recommendation**:
- Survivor's discretionary trust
- Beneficiaries: Spouse and children
- Trustees: Spouse and trusted friend/relative

**Rationale**:
- Survivor has control
- Can provide for family as needed
- Protects children's inheritance
- Flexible

### Scenario 2: Second Marriage

**Situation**:
- Remarried, children from first marriage
- Want to protect children's inheritance
- £250,000 life insurance

**Recommendation**:
- Discretionary trust
- Beneficiaries: "All my children"
- Trustees: Adult child from first marriage + friend
- Letter of wishes: Guidance on balancing current spouse needs vs children's inheritance

**Rationale**:
- Ensures children not disinherited
- Trustees can balance interests
- Current spouse provided for but doesn't control everything

### Scenario 3: Single Person

**Situation**:
- Single, no dependents
- Want proceeds to siblings
- £100,000 life insurance

**Recommendation**:
- Absolute trust (if equal split desired)
- Or discretionary trust (if flexible split preferred)
- Beneficiaries: Named siblings
- Trustees: 2 siblings

**Rationale**:
- Simple structure
- Avoids probate
- Faster payment
- IHT-efficient (outside estate)

### Scenario 4: Business Protection

**Situation**:
- Partnership, 3 partners
- Shareholder protection insurance
- £500,000 per partner

**Recommendation**:
- Absolute trust
- Beneficiaries: Other partners
- Trustees: Partners
- Cross-option agreement in place

**Rationale**:
- Proceeds go to partners to buy shares
- Certainty required (business context)
- Aligned with partnership agreement

## Trust Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. No Trust at All
- Most common mistake
- Payout in estate (IHT + probate delay)
- Always recommend trust

### 2. Wrong Trust Type
- Absolute when discretionary better
- No flexibility for changing circumstances

### 3. Poor Trustee Choice
- Only 1 trustee (risk if dies)
- All trustees older than policyholder
- Untrustworthy trustees

### 4. Not Updating
- Trustees die/become unsuitable
- Beneficiaries change (divorce, estrangement)
- Review every 3-5 years

### 5. No Letter of Wishes
- Trustees have no guidance
- Disputes among beneficiaries
- Your intentions unclear

### 6. Provider vs Solicitor Trust
- Solicitor trust rarely needed
- Provider trust usually sufficient
- Don't pay unnecessarily

### 7. Forgetting to Register
- TRS (Trust Registration Service) required
- Deadline: 90 days of becoming liable to tax
- Or 31 January following creation
- Penalties for non-compliance

## Trust Registration Service (TRS)

**What It Is**: HMRC register of trusts

**Who Must Register**:
- Discretionary trusts (usually)
- If income/gains arise
- Within 90 days of tax liability

**Exceptions**:
- Pure protection trusts (usually)
- If no income/gains before payout
- Pay out within reasonable period

**Practical Impact**:
- Most protection trusts: Don't need to register unless claim made
- If claim: Register before distributing (if tax liability)
- Provider may guide

**How to Register**:
- HMRC online portal
- Need trust details, trustees, beneficiaries
- Trustee responsibility

## Suitability

**Trust is Suitable For**:
- All protection policies (almost)
- Anyone with estate >£325,000 (IHT concern)
- Anyone wanting faster payout
- Anyone with beneficiaries who need protection
- Complex families

**Trust May NOT Be Needed**:
- Very simple estate (well below IHT threshold)
- No dependents or beneficiaries
- Policy for business purposes (shareholder protection - but still often used)

**Default Recommendation**: Always recommend trust unless clear reason not to

## Best Practice

**Good trust advice includes**:
- Explain benefits (IHT, probate, control)
- Recommend appropriate trust type
- Help choose suitable trustees
- Explain beneficiary options
- Provide or assist with letter of wishes
- Use provider trust (free, suitable)
- Confirm setup with client
- Remind to review every 3-5 years
- Document advice in suitability letter
